## 1. What a cut is

A cut is punctuation: "I am going to bring this idea to an end and start something new." [SB, Murch: "a shot presents us with an idea... and the cut is a 'blink' that separates and punctuates those ideas"]. Murch observed listeners blink when they "get" an idea, and cut near an actor's blink points in *The Conversation* [SB].

**Problem solved:** cuts that land mid-thought feel arbitrary; cuts that land when the thought completes feel invisible.

**Rules**
- Place the cut where the idea completes, not where a word ends. In voiceover: end of clause, or just before the next idea's first word (the J-cut position, `sound-design.md` section 5). [Synth, derived from Murch]
- Cutting after every line is what Murch called the "Dragnet" system: punchy but ignores how conversation actually flows. [SB] Use it deliberately (comic or rapid-fire), not by default.
- The cut does not *create* the blink moment; a well-placed cut punctuates one that already exists. [Murch]

**Fails when:** the content has no discrete ideas (continuous demonstration). Then cut on action or hold.

## 2. The priority ladder (Rule of Six)

Murch's six criteria for a cut, with his admittedly tongue-in-cheek weights [Murch]:

| # | Criterion | Weight | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Emotion | 51% | Is the cut true to the emotion of the moment? |
| 2 | Story | 23% | Does it advance the story? |
| 3 | Rhythm | 10% | Is it rhythmically "right"? |
| 4 | Eye-trace | 7% | Does attention land where the next shot expects it? |
| 5 | Planarity (2D screen) | 5% | Do axis / screen direction hold? |
| 6 | Spatial (3D) continuity | 4% | Are people/objects where they should be? |

Principle: "sacrifice your way up, item by item, from the bottom." Satisfying higher items tends to hide failures in lower items, not vice-versa. The top three are tightly bound. [Murch, SB]

**Operational use:** when two cut options conflict, score both on 1-3 first. Only use 4-6 to break ties or to explain why something feels "off" when 1-3 are fine.

**Limits:** the weights are not measured data; they come from one editor's feature-film practice. Treat as an ordering, not arithmetic. For explainers add Accuracy at level 1 [Synth].

**Apparent tension, reconciled** [Synth interpretation, not stated by either source]: Murch gives rhythm only 10% *as a criterion for a single cut*, yet is quoted saying editing is "70% about rhythm" [EFAP]. The two are compatible if the second refers to the editor's overall job - establishing "an interesting, coherent rhythm of emotion and thought" [SB, Murch] - while the first asks whether this particular cut point is rhythmically right. Emotion, story and rhythm are "extremely tightly connected" in practice [Murch]. Use: when a cut is wrong, look at the top three together before touching the bottom three.

## 3. Narrative skeleton for short explanatory video

[Synth; a common hook -> problem -> reveal -> meaning -> ending shape; adapt freely]

1. **Promise** - a visual or verbal question the viewer wants answered.
2. **Tension** - why the obvious answer is insufficient or wrong (open loop).
3. **Evidence** - show, don't just tell: a demonstration, measurement, diagram.
4. **Turn** - the one sentence/visual where understanding flips.
5. **Payoff** - the answer, exactly as strong as the promise.
6. **Landing** - one takeaway; a repeatable observation for the viewer; no filler outro.

**Contract test:** write the promise and the payoff as two sentences. If a skeptical viewer would say "that's not what you promised," rewrite one of them. If the honest payoff is "scientists still debate this," the promise must be phrased as a question about the *debate*, not a secret about the *answer*.

## 4. Emotion takes time

"Emotions take time. When we watch people on screen... we have time to watch their faces before they speak, and time to watch them afterwards. Editors have to decide: how much time do I give this emotion?" [EFAP]

- Evidence from the source: in the *Empire Strikes Back* example, shots shorten toward a peak (about 15 s up), then lengthen and are held for twice that long on the way down, "so that we, the audience, have time to feel Luke's failure." A comparable beat in a modern film took 30 frames versus Luke's ~30 seconds, and "did you believe that emotion?" was the point. [EFAP]
- "Four seconds makes a big difference... there are no right answers." [EFAP]

**Operational rules** [Synth from EFAP]
- After any beat meant to land (the "aha", a surprising number, a reveal), give the viewer a hold: at least one reading/comprehension unit with nothing new competing. Do not stack the next idea on top of the payoff.
- Build with progressively shorter shots, release with longer ones. The release is longer than the build.
- Some emotions play better in one continuous shot, others across multiple shots that "build up and come down." [EFAP] Decide per beat; do not default to coverage-and-cut.

## 5. Eyes, reactions, and what the viewer infers

- "More than any other factor, the eyes tell you the emotion of the scene." Cutting from a character's eyes to what they look at tells us what they are thinking without words. [EFAP]
- Editors look for "a change in the actor's eyes", e.g. a moment of decision. [EFAP]
- Caution (critique of the source): other cues (face, posture, head movement) also carry emotion; the cut itself carries meaning. [Open Culture comment thread, secondary] Do not reduce reaction shots to "show the eyes."

**Operational:** for a presenter, find the frame where thought changes (eyes shift, breath, pause) and cut near it. For non-human subjects (a lens, an eye diagram), the analogue is the moment attention should move: cut when the explanation *needs the next object*.

## 6. Meaning lives between shots

Juxtaposition makes meaning: two shots together communicate something neither does alone (Kuleshov / Eisenstein tradition). [SB mentions the Kuleshov effect]. Caveat: no film or photo documentation of Kuleshov's original experiment is known to survive and accounts differ [Wikipedia, secondary], so treat it as a working heuristic, not a proven law.

**Three legitimate roles for B-roll / insert shots** [Synth]
1. **Illustrate** - shows what the words say (use when the word is abstract and the image is concrete).
2. **Evidence** - shows something the words can't prove (measurement, before/after, the phenomenon itself).
3. **Metaphor** - maps an unfamiliar idea onto a familiar one. Must be *structurally* correct (see `motion-design.md` section 7), or it teaches the wrong model.

**Mute test:** play with the sound off. Can a viewer say what each shot is for? If a shot only "fills time", replace it with evidence or remove it and tighten.
**Redundancy test:** if the B-roll repeats the words exactly (say "magnifying glass", show a magnifying glass), keep it only if the image arrives *before or after* the word to add something.

## 7. Invisible vs. deliberate cuts

Classical editing cuts with the rhythm so the cut is invisible. But "some emotions play better if you cut in a jarring way", e.g. agitation; others play better if you cut to make the audience uncomfortable. "What really matters is what reaction you want from people." [EFAP] Schoonmaker: sometimes "hold just a little bit too long... and then make a cut, if it's justified." [EFAP]

**Decision rule** [Synth]
- Default: invisible (cut on thought, action, or beat).
- Break it only when you can name the reaction (urgency, unease, disorientation) and the content supports it. If you cannot name the reaction, do not break the rule.

## 8. How to find what to remove

1. Mark every beat's job (emotion, info, rhythm, attention). Remove beats with no job.
2. Remove redundancy: the second time the viewer is told the same thing.
3. Remove setup that the payoff doesn't use ("Chekhov's graphic").
4. Remove the strongest-looking element if the idea works without it (restraint test).
5. When test viewers say "this part was boring," don't automatically cut it: audiences "are good at noticing when something isn't working. But they aren't good at pointing out why"; the cause is often missing information, not excess length. [SB] Check comprehension first.

## 9. Working method

- Watch the material as the audience will; Murch avoids set visits to see only what's on screen. [SB] Practical equivalent: do a first pass of footage with no plan in mind, note what *you* felt, then plan.
- Pull one representative frame per take/shot to speed selection. [SB]
- "If you watch anything over and over again, you eventually feel the moment when the shot wants you to cut." [EFAP] Practice is the only teacher; the skill's job is to make intuition inspectable, not to replace it.
- One cut at a time (Kahn). [EFAP]

## 10. Common mistakes -> corrections

| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Cutting at every sentence end | Dragnet rhythm; viewer fatigues | Vary: overlap (J/L cut), hold reaction, merge two short ideas |
| Payoff immediately followed by next point | No time to feel/understand | Add hold, or let a graphic/visual carry the payoff in silence |
| Hook promises more than evidence supports | Trust collapse | Rewrite promise to the honest scope (section 3 contract test) |
| Metaphor visual contradicts the science | Teaches a wrong model | Rebuild to match the physics (see `motion-design.md`) |
| Decorative B-roll | No job | Replace with evidence or delete |
| Fixing the "boring" scene test viewers named | Real problem is missing info | Diagnose comprehension first |
